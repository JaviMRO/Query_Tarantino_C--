# Stage 1 benchmark analysis (C++)

This document interprets the full benchmark run of the C++ implementation of October 3, 2026
(`benchmarks/report/comparison.md`, generated by `scripts/compare_results.py` from `benchmarks/results/cpp-*.csv`). It
answers the same four questions as the analysis of the Java implementation:

- why each structure gets its numbers, given the complexity of its operations;
- whether the benchmark is representative;
- what would happen with tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands and a million books;
- which structure to choose for each component.

The rule for choosing is: **the structure with the best complexity in the operations that grow with the number of books;
on a tie, the one with the best measured constants.**

`benchmarks/` is not in git, so this document keeps the numbers it relies on.

## 1. Summary

| Component | Choice | By complexity | What the measurements say |
|---|---|---|---|
| Datalake | `batch` | Stores and looks up in O(1), with O(N/1 000) directories | Ties with `book` (2 265 against 1 995 books/s). `time` looks up in O(N): 0.36 ms per book with 1 000 books |
| Inverted index | `mongo` | Never worse than the other two; memory, opening and freshness in O(1) | `json` wins 13 of the 18 metrics with 1 000 books, but its memory, opening and reloading are O(P): ~29 GB of memory with 100 000 books |
| Metadata | `sqlite` | Same as `mongo` in every operation | Reads 46 times faster (2.97 against 138 µs by id) |

Four findings that `comparison.md` does not show:

1. **All three index structures rewrite almost the whole index on every batch.** A batch of 100 books touches only 14% of
   the terms, but those terms (among them every frequent one) hold 86% of the postings, and the share stays between 85%
   and 93% for every batch up to 2 800 books. Updating costs O(N) per batch in all three, and reaching N books costs
   O(N²).
2. **`mongo` has the most expensive updates.** `$addToSet` compares each new id with every id already in the list
   (checked in MongoDB 7.0's source), so a batch of 100 books costs 408 ns per touched posting, 4.5 times more than a
   single book (91 ns), against 209 ns in `folders` and 21 ns per posting rewritten in `json`. With 100 000 books `mongo`
   would take ~2.2 s per book.
3. **Queries have the same complexity in all three, and in C++ building the results weighs.** Copying each list into a
   `std::set` in `SearchCommand` costs O(df·log df) in all three; on top of it, the benchmark builds a metadata record for
   every result, ~30 ns each, a third of a frequent query with 1 000 books. That is why `mixed` (two terms, 2 results) is
   cheaper than `frequent` (one term, ~924 results). With 100 000 books, a frequent query would take 14 ms (json), 18 ms
   (folders) and 39 ms (mongo).
4. **In a real search, metadata dominates,** because it is fetched one result at a time. With 100 000 books, a frequent
   query would spend 0.27 s (sqlite) or ~13 s (mongo) reading metadata, against 11–36 ms in the index.

## 2. Data, method and notation

### 2.1 The run

- **Machine:** laptop with an Intel Core Ultra 9 275HX (24 cores: 8 performance and 16 efficient, one thread each; 36 MB
  of L3 cache), Windows 11 with WSL 2: Ubuntu 26.04 on Linux 6.6, 24 virtual processors, 15.3 GB of RAM and an ext4
  virtual disk.
- **Software:** GCC 15.2 in release mode (`-O2 -DNDEBUG`), ICU 78.2 (Unicode 17.0), SQLite 3.53.4, nlohmann/json 3.11.3
  and mongocxx 4.6.0. MongoDB 7.0.40 running in the same WSL 2 virtual machine, with a 1 GB cache.
- **Sizes:** 100, 300 and 1 000 books from `benchmarks/cache/`, in the order of `workload/book_ids.txt`. Updates add the next
  100 books (positions 1 001–1 100).
- **Harness:** the benchmark executables' own (SPEC §11): two passes of one process per benchmark, structure and size; the
  second runs the parameters in reverse order. Each value is the mean ± the half-width of its 95% confidence interval. Two
  structures tie when their intervals overlap.
- **Duration:** about 51 min (crawler 5 min, indexer 36 min, query 9.5 min).
- **Correctness:** the benchmark checks that the three structures hold as many terms as the books have and that every
  query returns the same books as a reference computed from the books themselves, before measuring it.

The Java analysis was made on a different machine (a MacBook Air M4, with MongoDB installed natively), so its numbers
and these cannot be compared one by one: only the conclusions can (section 10).

Besides the run, this document uses measurements made apart on the same machine, with the same build and cache, later
the same day: the vocabulary recount of section 2.3, and two small programs linked to the project's libraries that time
the parts of a build (reading and tokenizing, adding, writing; 3 repetitions) and of a query (with and without building
its results). Their absolute times varied up to 40% between repetitions (section 8), so they are used for proportions
and for constants that the run cannot separate, and are marked "measured apart".

### 2.3 How V and P grow

The vocabulary was recounted with the project's own `GutenbergText` and `Tokenizer`, on 2 800 books: the 1 100 of the
run's cache and the next 1 700 that `scripts/fill_cache.sh` adds, copied into a separate cache (its first 1 100 books are
the run's, byte for byte). The recount reproduces the benchmark's `term_count` exactly: 107 231, 224 907 and 435 391
terms.

| N | 100 | 300 | 1 000 | 2 000 | 2 800 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| V(N) | 107 231 | 224 907 | 435 391 | 587 929 | 755 845 |
| P(N) | 625 071 | 1 881 995 | 6 425 000 | 12 240 589 | 17 023 603 |
| P(N) / N | 6 251 | 6 273 | 6 425 | 6 120 | 6 080 |

These datasets are almost, but not exactly, the Java run's: they have 0.1–0.8% more terms (107 231 against 106 410 with
100 books, 435 391 against 434 996 with 1 000) and exactly 580 fewer postings with 100, 300 and 1 000 books. The two
caches were copied on different days and differ somewhere in their first 100 books. The rest of this section's figures
match the Java analysis's to within 0.1% (the batch below touches the same 78 643 terms), and with 1 000 books there are
the same 426 authors for 974 books that SPEC §11 gives.

- **Heaps' law:** V(N) ≈ 7 984·N^0.573, fitted on 10 sizes between 100 and 2 800 books (100, 200, 300, 500, 700, 1 000,
  1 500, 2 000, 2 500 and 2 800). With only N ≥ 1 000 the exponent is 0.543. The vocabulary grows more slowly than N: each
  new book adds fewer and fewer new terms.
- **Postings:** P(N) ≈ 6 200·N (fitted exponent 0.987). Each book adds about 6 200 postings, one per distinct term it
  contains.
- **Zipf's law:** with 1 000 books, 54% of the terms appear in a single book, and a few terms hold almost all the postings.
  No term appears in every book; the most common one (`one`) is in 961 of 1 000.

| Terms that appear in… | How many | % of the vocabulary | % of the postings |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ≥ 50% of the books | 2 394 | 0.5% | 25% |
| ≥ 10% of the books | 14 860 | 3.4% | 68% |
| ≥ 1% of the books | 54 942 | 12.6% | 88% |

- **A batch of 100 new books** touches 78 643 terms. Of these, 16 850 are new and 61 793 were already in the index: 14% of
  the vocabulary. Those 61 793 terms hold **86% of the postings**, because every frequent term is among them. With 2 800
  books the batch is no exception: every batch of 100 consecutive books touches between 85% and 93% of the postings of the
  index it is added to (86–90% from 800 books on). This decides the complexity of updates (section 5).

### 2.4 How the estimates are made

Each estimate is the complexity of the operation with its measured constants. For example, the memory of the `json`
index is a cost per term plus a cost per posting, c_V·V(N) + c_P·P(N).

- The constants are fitted with 100 and 1 000 books.
- Each model is validated by predicting 300 books, a size not used to fit it. Errors range from 0% to 11%, with two
  exceptions: the books of an author in sqlite (−18%), whose samples come in two levels (section 8), and the queries
  (−24%), whose cost per id did not yet grow with log df at these sizes, so their estimates are on the high side.
- Larger sizes use the V(N) and P(N) of section 2.3: the two fits of V give a range.
- The full build is not modelled from V and P: they grow together between 100 and 1 000 books and a fit cannot tell them
  apart (for `json` it gives a negative cost per term). Its parts were measured apart instead (section 5.3).
- Where a constant grew between 100 and 1 000 books, as the cost of adding a posting to the in-memory sets does
  (section 5.3), the estimates keep the 1 000-book value: they are lower bounds.

Curves are not fitted blindly. With 1 000 books fixed costs weigh a lot, and a curve y = a·N^b fitted to the three points
is misleading. For example, mongo's mean query time grows only 1.6 times from 100 to 1 000 books (436 to 704 µs), because
a round trip of ~226 µs per term dominates it. Fitted to the mean query times, such a curve predicts that with a million
books mongo would answer about 40 times faster than json. With the complexity model, a frequent query takes ~0.16 s in
json and ~0.41 s in mongo.

The figures for 100 000 and 1 000 000 books are orders of magnitude, not exact predictions. Project Gutenberg has about
75 000 books: the 100 000 column is all of Gutenberg, and the million column assumes other sources.

## 3. Is the benchmark representative?

**Yes, for comparing these three implementations up to 1 000 books:**

- all of them process the same books with the same vocabulary, and this is checked;
- the harness measures with warm-up, two processes, confidence intervals and reverse order in the second pass;
- the constants it measures (cost per term, per posting and per file) are what make extrapolation possible.

**No, for a product with tens of thousands of books or more:**

1. **Everything fits in memory.** With 1 000 books, `json` takes 370 MB in the query service and 364 MB in the indexer,
   and `mongo` 58 MB inside its 1 GB cache. With 100 000 books none of that fits, and disk reads and memory exhaustion
   appear, which the benchmark never sees.
2. **Queries always touch the same 30 terms.** The 30 queries in `workload/queries.txt` use 30 distinct terms, so their
   lists (or their 30 files, in folders) are always cached. A real search engine receives a wide variety of terms.
3. **Fixed costs weigh too much.** mongo's ~226 µs per term dominate with 1 000 books and are small next to reading
   ~92 000 ids with 100 000 books (section 5.5).
4. **MongoDB runs in the same virtual machine as the benchmark.** SPEC §11 asks for MongoDB on the machine itself, not
   inside a virtual machine, so that no network hop separates them; there is none here, as both run inside WSL 2, but
   each round trip took 100–230 µs, two to five times the 47–48 µs that the Java run measured with MongoDB installed
   natively on a Mac. With MongoDB on another machine, as in production, it would cost a few tenths of a millisecond:
   about what it costs here.
5. **One query is measured at a time, without metadata or HTTP.** There are no concurrent queries and no queries while
   indexing, and the complete search is not measured: `query_time` uses constant metadata on purpose. In C++ that
   constant metadata is not free: building the record of each result costs ~30 ns, a third of a frequent query with
   1 000 books (section 5.4).
6. **The full build keeps the whole index in memory before writing it,** in all three structures (`build_memory`:
   356–364 MB with 1 000 books, 48 B per posting). The C++ processes have no memory limit, so on this machine the
   benchmark could run up to about 45 000 books. The real pipeline writes every 100 books, so that memory only grows
   without bound with json.
7. **Updates start from an index just loaded.** Each `json` update loads the index from its file, which lays out each
   list's nodes one after another in memory: rewriting it then costs 21 ns per posting. An indexer that keeps adding
   batches to the same in-memory index ends up with the layout of a build, which took 8–14 times longer to write (about
   250 ns per posting, section 5.3).
8. **It compares implementations, not technologies.** Here "json" means "the whole index in memory, loaded at once and
   rewritten whole", and "mongo" means "one document per term that grows with every book". Another design with the same
   technology would give other numbers.
9. **It was measured on a laptop with a hybrid processor, under WSL 2.** Some configurations differ by up to 50% from one
   process to another (section 8).

For Stage 1 this is enough, as long as the report states these limits and accompanies the results with this scale
analysis.

## 4. Datalake

### 4.1 How each structure stores the books

- `time`: `YYYYMMDD/HH/<id>.header.txt` and `<id>.body.txt`, by download hour (the layout of the assignment, PDF §3).
- `book`: one directory per book, `<id>/header.txt` and `body.txt`.
- `batch`: one directory per 1 000 ids, `<id div 1000>/<id>.header.txt` and `<id>.body.txt`.

Before storing a book, the crawler checks whether it is already stored (`IngestBookCommand::stored`), so storing includes
a lookup. For a book that is not stored yet, that lookup finds nothing.

### 4.2 Complexity

H is the number of hours with downloads: it grows with how long the crawl runs, not with N.

| Operation | time | book | batch | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Look up a book | O(N) | O(1) | O(1) | `time` does not know in which hour the book was stored and walks the tree (`platform::files::find`, up to depth 3) until it meets the body. `book` and `batch` build the path from the id and check that the body exists |
| Store a book | O(N) | O(1) | O(1) | Includes the lookup above, which for a new book walks the whole datalake |
| Detect new books | O(H + new) | O(N) | O(N) | `time` lists only the hour directories and reads those after the last check. `book` and `batch` check the date of every book |
| Recover from a crash | O(N) | O(N) | O(N) | All three walk the datalake (`BookFiles::removeIncompleteWrites`) looking for half-written files |
| Directories | O(H) | O(N) | O(N/1 000) | |
| Files and disk | O(N) | O(N) | O(N) | Two files per book with the same content |

### 4.3 The measurements confirm the complexity

| | N = 100 | N = 300 | N = 1 000 | With 10 times more books |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Look up, `time` | 46.2 µs | 118 µs | 364 µs | ×7.9: O(N), plus a fixed 11 µs |
| Look up, `batch` | 0.55 µs | 0.55 µs | 0.57 µs | ×1.04: O(1) |
| Look up, `book` | 0.54 µs | 0.55 µs | 0.59 µs | ×1.09: O(1) |
| Store, `time` | 1 382 books/s | 1 605 books/s | 1 185 books/s | Drops once the lookup starts to weigh |
| Store, `batch` | 1 481 books/s | 2 049 books/s | 2 265 books/s | Does not drop |
| Detect new, `time` | 0.031 ms | 0.032 ms | 0.035 ms | Constant |
| Detect new, `batch` | 0.14 ms | 0.30 ms | 0.85 ms | Grows with N |

- **Constants:**
  - A lookup in `time` costs 10.8 µs plus 0.35 µs per stored book (validated with 300 books: −1.3%).
  - Storing in `time` costs, on top of what `batch` costs, 0.80 µs per book already stored (−8.6%): the lookup of a new book
    finds nothing and walks the whole tree, twice as far as a lookup that succeeds, which stops halfway on average.
  - Detecting new books costs 0.66 µs (`book`) and 0.79 µs (`batch`) per stored book (+3.8% and 0.0%).
- **Why `book` and `batch` store faster with more books:** the first books of the cache are larger (605 KB on average for
  the first 100, 482 KB for the first 1 000), and each run has a fixed part of ~14 ms. Storing costs 14 ms plus 0.89 ms per
  MB of text in `batch` (+3.6%).
- **Recovery:** all three structures recover correctly from a crash (`recovery_ok` = 1 and no leftover files, with 100
  books).
- **Disk:** they take the same space, 454 MB with 1 000 books (459 MB in whole blocks).

### 4.4 Estimates

| | 1 000 (measured) | 10 000 | 20 000 | 100 000 | 1 000 000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Look up a book, `time` | 0.36 ms | 3.5 ms | 7.1 ms | 35 ms | 0.35 s |
| Store N books into an empty datalake, `time` | 1 185 books/s | 225 books/s | 118 books/s | 25 books/s | 2.5 books/s |
| Time spent only looking up to store N books, `time` | 0.4 s | 40 s | 2.7 min | 1.1 h | 4.6 days |
| Look up a book, `book` and `batch` | < 1 µs | < 1 µs | < 1 µs | < 1 µs | < 1 µs |
| Detect new, `book` and `batch` | 0.7–0.85 ms | 6.6–7.9 ms | 13–16 ms | 66–79 ms | 0.66–0.79 s |
| Directories, `book` | 1 000 | 10 000 | 20 000 | 100 000 | 1 000 000 |
| Directories, `batch` | 2 | ~10 | ~20 | ~100 | ~1 000 |
| Disk (all three) | 0.45 GB | 4.5 GB | 9 GB | 45 GB | 454 GB |

### 4.5 Choice: `batch`

- **Complexity:** stores and looks up in O(1), like `book`, with far fewer directories: O(N/1 000) against O(N). For all of
  Gutenberg that would be about 75 directories instead of 75 000.
- **Why not `time`:** its lookup is O(N). To store 100 000 books it would spend ~1.1 hours just looking up, and it would
  store 25 books/s on average, against ~2 000 in `batch`.
- **`time`'s only advantage is not needed:** detecting new books in O(H + new). The control service already queues each book
  for indexing as soon as it finishes downloading. To detect new books without the control layer, a manifest (a file to
  which each stored id is appended) would be enough, and detection would become O(new) with any structure.

## 5. Inverted index

### 5.1 How each structure stores the index

- **json** (`MonolithicJsonIndexAdapter`): the whole index in memory, as a `std::map<std::string, std::set<int>>`. Every
  write (`flush`) serializes the whole index, as a stream, to a single JSON file (`JsonIndexFile`). The reader
  (`MonolithicJsonIndexReader`) loads the whole file the first time, with nlohmann/json's SAX parser, into a hash map of
  hash sets, and never reads it again.
- **folders** (`FolderPerTermIndexAdapter`): one file per term, one id per line, inside one directory per first letter
  (`TermFiles`). The postings added since the last write wait in `PendingPostings`, a hash map from term to a `std::set`
  of ids. For every term touched, each write reads its whole file into a `std::set`, adds the new ids, writes it whole to
  a temporary file and renames it.
- **mongo** (`MongodbIndexAdapter`): one `{term, postings[]}` document per term, with a unique index on `term`. Each write
  sends one unordered bulk write with one `$addToSet` per term touched, and MongoDB compares the new ids with those already
  in the array.
- **Query** (`SearchCommand`, shared by all three): for each term it fetches its list as a hash set, copies it into a
  `std::set` and keeps in the first set only the ids present in each of the others (`std::erase_if`). Then it fetches the
  metadata of every result, one by one.

### 5.2 Complexity

Σ_touched df is the number of postings of the terms a batch touches: ≈ 0.86·P (section 2.3).

| Operation | json | folders | mongo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build with N books | O(P·log N) | O(P·log N + V·c_fs) | O(P·log N + V·log V) |
| Update a batch of K books | O(P + V) | O(T_batch·c_fs + Σ_touched df·log df) | O(T_batch·log V + Σ_touched k·df) |
| Index N books in batches | O(N²/K) | O(N²·log N / K) | O(N²) |
| Open or reload the index | O(P + V) | O(1) | O(1) |
| Memory in the process | O(P + V) | O(1) | O(1) |
| Disk | O(P + V) | O(P + V·4 KB) | O(P + V) |
| Number of files | 1 | V | MongoDB's own, independent of V |
| Query with q terms (current algorithm) | O(Σ df·log df + R) | O(Σ (c_fs + df·log df) + R) | O(Σ (log V + df·log df) + R) |
| See new books | Reload: O(P + V) | O(1) | O(1) |

What matters in the table:

- **Updating is O(N) per batch in all three.**
  - json rewrites everything (P + V).
  - folders and mongo only touch the batch's terms, but those terms hold 86% of the postings, so Σ_touched df ≈ 0.86·P.
  - folders reads each touched list into a `std::set`, which adds a log df; merging the sorted file with the new ids
    would avoid it.
  - mongo's `$addToSet` compares every new id with the whole list (MongoDB 7.0, `addtoset_node.cpp`: a loop over the
    existing array for each element to add), so each touched list costs k(t)·df(t). A batch of 100 books adds on average
    31 ids to each list it touches (weighted by its length), and k grows with K: batching divides the cost per book of
    json and folders by K, but not mongo's comparisons, which is why indexing N books costs O(N²) whatever K is.
  - The advantage the assignment attributes to folders ("only the file of the affected term is modified") barely helps: a
    batch always touches the frequent terms, and their lists grow with N.
- **Memory, opening and freshness separate json from the other two:** O(P + V) against O(1).
- **Queries have the same complexity in all three.** `SearchCommand` copies each whole list into a `std::set`, which costs
  O(df·log df) per term: much more than reaching the term (O(1) in json, O(log V) in mongo). Then it builds one result
  per book found, O(R).

### 5.3 Measured constants

**The build, part by part** (with 1 000 books, measured apart; `mongo`'s write is the run's time minus the other parts):

| Part of the build | json | folders | mongo |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Read, split and tokenize the books (shared) | 5.3 s | 5.3 s | 5.3 s |
| Add the postings to the in-memory sets | 19.5 s | 14.2 s | 14.2 s (same code as folders) |
| Write: sort the terms and flush | 1.6 s | 18.4 s | ~17.5 s |
| Sum of the parts | 26.4 s | 37.9 s | |
| `full_build_time` in the run | 28.3 ± 1.4 s | 36.8 ± 2.8 s | 37.0 ± 0.9 s |

**The other constants:**

| Constant | json | folders | mongo | Validation with N = 300 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Build: add one posting (100 → 300 → 1 000 books) | 1.1 → 1.8 → 3.0 µs | 0.6 → 1.6 → 2.2 µs | same as folders | measured apart |
| Build: write | 0.25 µs per posting | 41 µs per term file (22 µs with 100 books) | ~39 µs per term | measured apart |
| Query: fixed cost per term | 0.36 µs | 2.4 µs | 226 µs | |
| Query: reading each id of the list | — | 44 ns | 268 ns | |
| Query: copying the list into a `std::set` (shared) | 7.1 ns × df·log₂ df | same | same | −24% |
| Query: building each result with the constant metadata (shared) | ~30 ns | same | same | measured apart: 20–50 ns |
| Memory in the process | 195 B per term + 44 B per posting | 0 | 0 | −0.3% |
| Memory while building (all three) | 128 B per term + 48 B per posting | 109 B per term + 48 B per posting | same as folders | 0.0% / +0.1% |
| Disk | 4.1 B per term + 4.6 B per posting | 4 KB per term + 4.5 B per posting | 22 B per term + 7.6 B per posting | −3.5% to −7.8% |
| Open | 9 ms + 134 ns per posting | — | — | +1.7% |
| Update a batch: fixed part | 22.5 ms per book (15.4 with 100 books) | 28 ms per book | 22.7 ms per book | |
| Update a batch: per posting rewritten | 21 ns (whole index) | 209 ns (the 86% touched) | 408 ns (the 86% touched) | folders −2.9%, mongo +2.1% |
| Update a single book: per posting rewritten | 21 ns (whole index) | 118 ns (the lists of its terms) | 91 ns (the lists of its terms) | −11%, −7.9%, +1.7% |

What the constants say:

- **Adding postings to `std::set` trees dominates the build** (74% of json's, ~38% of folders' and mongo's). Each
  `std::set` is a red-black tree whose nodes are allocated one by one as the books arrive, scattered over the heap. With
  100 books the sets (~43 MB) partly fit in the processor's 36 MB cache; with 1 000 (~360 MB) every level of the tree is a
  trip to memory, and the cost per posting grew 2.7 (json) to 3.5 times (folders and mongo). json pays it twice: it also
  looks each term up in a `std::map` (log₂ V ≈ 19 levels).
- **Tokenizing is a fifth of json's build** (5.3 ms per book: ICU lowercasing and a hash map of terms per book), and the
  same in all three.
- **folders pays ~22–41 µs per term file** (creating, writing and renaming it): 17.9 s of the 37.9 s measured apart with
  1 000 books. ext4 is far cheaper than APFS here: the Java run paid ~147 µs per term file on the Mac.
- **mongo pays ~226 µs per term on every query.** That is the round trip to MongoDB: the same order as looking up a book's
  metadata by id in mongo (105–230 µs).
- **json takes 44 B of memory per posting in the query service**, the node of a `std::unordered_set<int>` and its share of
  buckets, and 48 B while building, the node of a `std::set<int>`. In the file it takes 4.6 B: 10 times less.
- **json rewrites at ~21 ns per posting** (~220 MB/s) after loading the index from its file, as the update benchmarks do,
  and at ~250 ns per posting right after building it in memory (measured apart: 124–187 ms against 1.6–1.8 s with 1 000
  books), because loading allocates each list's nodes one after another. It is fast, but it rewrites the whole index on
  every batch.
- **The fixed part of json's updates grows with N** (15.4, 18.3 and 22.5 ms per book with 100, 300 and 1 000 books):
  adding 100 books to a larger in-memory index is slower, for the same reason as in the build.
- **mongo's batches pay 4.5 times more per touched posting than single books** (408 against 91 ns): with ~31 new ids per
  touched list, `$addToSet` compares each of them with the whole list, about 11 ns per comparison, on top of ~80 ns per
  posting for the rest (reading and rewriting the document).

- Indexing in batches of 100 makes each book 6.4 (json), 8.8 (folders) and 5.8 (mongo) times cheaper. mongo gains least

- Opening folders and mongo only creates a reader: the time measured is answering the first query (`love`, in 888 books).
- The "0" memory of folders and mongo (6 KB) means the memory is outside the process: in the file system cache and in
  MongoDB's cache (up to 1 GB).

The query categories show how the cost adds up:

| Query (µs) | df of its terms | json | folders | mongo |

  452 ≈ 2 × 226).
- In mongo, `mixed` ≈ `frequent` + `rare` (735 against 781), as in the Java analysis.
- In json and folders, `mixed` is cheaper than `frequent` alone (64.9 against 94.6 µs in json), although it does strictly
  more work in the index: a second term and an intersection. The difference is the results: `frequent` returns ~924 books
  and `mixed` 2, and the benchmark builds a metadata record for each (a `BookMetadata` with four strings, appended to a
  vector). Measured apart, the same frequent queries took 36–43 µs without building their results and 60–85 µs building
  them: 20–50 ns per result. In the run, the difference between `frequent` and `mixed` is 30 µs, ~32 ns per result.
- `long` also pays the intersections: `std::erase_if` checks each id of the first list against each of the other three.

### 5.6 What the estimates show

1. **Memory is json's limit.** The C++ services set no maximum, so they can use the whole machine: 15.3 GB here.
   - The indexer and the query service with json, each with its own copy of the index, would run out of memory at about
     25 000 books; the query service alone at about 53 000.
   - With 100 000 books, each copy would need ~29–30 GB.
2. **Indexing in batches costs O(N²) in all three, but with very different constants.** To reach 100 000 books:
   - json would take 2.4 h rewriting an index just loaded, and up to ~22 h with the memory layout of one built batch
     after batch, if it had 30 GB of memory;
   - folders, ~16 h: it pays ~28 ms per book and 209 ns per touched posting, 10 times json's rewriting;
   - mongo, ~31 h: `$addToSet` makes it pay 408 ns per touched posting, 20 times json's rewriting.
3. **Queries end up close.** With 100 000 books, a frequent query takes 14 ms (json), 18 ms (folders) and 39 ms (mongo):
   the copy into a `std::set`, shared by all three, dominates json and folders; mongo also reads ~92 000 ids from the
   document at 268 ns each. json's advantage on short lists (0.36 µs per term against 226 µs) disappears when the lists
   are long.
4. **folders does not scale in number of files.** With 100 000 books there would be 5–6 million files, with hundreds of
   thousands in the directory of each common letter, and 24–27 GB allocated for ~3–4 GB of data.
5. **mongo holds up to ~1.4 million books.** Beyond that, a term present in almost every book would exceed the 16 MB a
   MongoDB document allows. Until then, its process memory, opening and freshness do not depend on N, but its updates are
   the most expensive of the three from ~1 000 books on.

### 5.8 Choice: `mongo`

Complexity per operation, with K fixed (= is the same as the best of the three):

| Operation | json | folders | mongo |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Build | = | = | = |
| Update in batches | = | worse, O(P·log N) per batch | = |
| Query (current algorithm) | = | = | = |
| Open or reload | worse, O(P + V) | = | = |
| Memory in the process | worse, O(P + V) | = | = |
| See new books | worse, O(P + V) | = | = |
| Number of files | = | worse, O(V) | = |

- **mongo never has worse complexity than the other two.**
- **json is worse in three operations,** the ones that prevent using it at scale: memory, opening and freshness.
- **folders is worse in number of files** and, as written, in updates, because it reads each touched list into a
  `std::set`.

Between json and mongo, json has better constants (20 times better per posting in updates, 600 times in reaching a
term), but the rule puts complexity first. Besides, json cannot reach the sizes where those constants would matter.

mongo's constant in updates is the worst of the three, and grows with the batch size: before it indexes beyond a few tens
of thousands of books, its postings should be split into documents of bounded size (section 9), which also lifts the
16 MB limit.

Only one operation would favor json with a better algorithm: intersecting starting from the shortest list and checking its
ids in the others, which costs O(q·df_min) if the lists are in memory. folders and mongo, as they are, have to read each
whole list: O(Σ df). That is why the target design combines both (section 9).

## 6. Metadata

### 6.1 How each one stores the metadata

- **sqlite** (`SqliteMetadataAdapter`, `SqliteMetadataReader`, SQLite's C API): table `books` with `book_id INTEGER PRIMARY
  KEY` (a B-tree). Each book is stored with a prepared `INSERT OR REPLACE` in autocommit mode, that is, one transaction,
  with its write synced to disk, per book.
- **mongo** (`MongodbMetadataAdapter`, `MongodbMetadataReader`): collection `books` with a unique index on `book_id`. Each
  book is stored with a `replace_one` with upsert.
- **Books by an author:** sqlite uses `WHERE author LIKE '%author%'`, and mongo an unanchored, case-insensitive regular
  expression. Neither search can use an index, and there is no index on `author` either.

### 6.2 Complexity

r is the number of books returned.

| Operation | sqlite | mongo |
|---|---|---|
| Book by id | O(log N) | O(log N) |
| Books by an author ("contains") | O(N) | O(N) |
| Books by an author with an index (exact or prefix) | O(log N + r) | O(log N + r) |
| Store a book | O(log N) + one transaction with a synced write | O(log N) + one round trip |

They have the same complexity in every operation, so the constants decide.

### 6.3 Measured constants

| | sqlite | mongo |
|---|---:|---:|
| Book by id (does not change with N) | 2.8–3.0 µs | 138–193 µs |
| Books by an author: fixed part + each book scanned | 6.0 µs + 46 ns | 277 µs + 836 ns |
| Store 1 000 books | 3 565 ± 137 ms | 320 ± 5 ms |

- **By id:** sqlite runs in the same process; mongo pays the round trip, which took 100–230 µs in this run.
- **By author:** sqlite scans the table 18 times faster.
- **Storing is not a fair comparison:**
  - sqlite confirms each book with a write synced to disk, 3.6 ms per book on WSL 2's virtual disk (0.46 ms on the Mac of
    the Java run);
  - MongoDB, with its default configuration, acknowledges before writing its journal: 0.30 ms per book;
  - with one transaction per batch, sqlite would take a few milliseconds per batch;
  - here it is not negligible: 3.6 s for 1 000 books, against 23–35 s to index them in batches. In the real pipeline,
    which saves each book's metadata as it indexes it, it adds 8–15% to each batch.

### 6.5 Choice: `sqlite`

sqlite has the same complexity as mongo in every operation, reads 46 times faster and needs no server.

Recommended changes:

- One transaction per batch when storing: on this machine it removes ~3.6 ms per book.
- A column with the normalized author and an index on it, or FTS5 to search words of the author or title. Searching by
  author then goes from O(N) to O(log N + r).

**Limit: one writer at a time.** SQLite locks the whole database file to write, so only one process writes at a time.
That is enough today, because the control layer is the only one that writes metadata (SPEC §9). With several indexers
writing at once, as in Stage 2 or with several containers:

- **On the same machine**, their writes queue up: each one waits for the lock, up to the 3 s `busy_timeout` the adapters
  set, and then fails with `SQLITE_BUSY`. WAL mode lets reads go on during a write, but there is still only one writer.
- **On several machines**, they cannot share the file: SQLite's locks are not reliable over a network file system, and
  the database can be corrupted.

For a distributed architecture, the logical long-term step is to move the metadata to **PostgreSQL**. Like MongoDB it
is a server, but relational, and with row locks and MVCC many processes write and read at the same time. It keeps the
same complexity: a B-tree on `book_id`, and `INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE` for the upsert. A trigram index (`pg_trgm`)
would also let the "contains" search by author use an index instead of scanning the table. The price is the one mongo
pays here: one network round trip per query, so a book by id would cost about mongo's 100–230 µs rather than sqlite's
3 µs. Fetching each page's metadata in a single query (section 7) makes that negligible. PostgreSQL has not been measured
here: it should be benchmarked as a third metadata backend before switching.

## 7. The complete search

With the current design, a query costs:

T(query) ≈ Σ per term (fixed cost + reading per id × df + 7.1 ns × df·log₂ df) + R × (metadata of one book by id)

With 100 000 books, a query for one frequent term (df ≈ R ≈ 92 400) splits like this:

| Part | json + sqlite | mongo + sqlite | mongo + mongo |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Index | 11 ms | 36 ms | 36 ms |
| Metadata of every result | 0.27 s | 0.27 s | ~13 s (10–20 s) |
| Total | ~0.28 s | ~0.31 s | ~13 s |

If results are paginated (for example, 20 per page) and the page's metadata is fetched in a single query, metadata costs one
query (~0.1–0.3 ms) and the index dominates again. That is why pagination is the improvement with the most impact for
Stage 2.

## 8. Reliability of the measurements

- **Datalake and queries:** the most stable measurements: ±1–3% in the queries of folders and mongo, and ±1–5% in the
  datalake, except two configurations with one slow run (`time`'s lookup with 300 books, ±9%, and `batch`'s write with
  1 000, ±14%). json's queries with 1 000 books took 70–72 µs in every iteration of the first pass and 75–127 µs in the
  second (±16%).
- **Differences between processes:** each configuration runs in two processes, one per pass, and some of them differ by
  30–50%, more than the iterations of one process:
  - json's update book by book took 31–51% longer in the second pass at every size, and mongo's 16–19% less;
  - mongo's build with 100 books took 48% longer in the second pass;
  - sqlite's book by id came in two levels, ~2.3 and 2.7–4.3 µs, and mongo's in two levels too, ~105 and ~220 µs, which
    changed between processes and even within one; sqlite's books by an author with 100 books took 8.8 µs in one process
    and 10–15 µs in the other;
  - the parts measured apart for this document varied up to 40% between repetitions (tokenizing 100 books: 0.58–0.99 s).

  The likely causes are the hybrid processor, whose performance and efficient cores Linux cannot tell apart under WSL 2,
  and its power management; this has not been checked. The second pass is not systematically slower: the passes took
  2 min 42 s and 2 min 41 s (crawler), 19 and 17 min (indexer) and 6 and 3.5 min (query), the first ones longer because they
  build the indexes the updates and queries start from.
- **The winners do not change:** with 1 000 books json's build interval does not overlap the others' (json 26.9–29.7 s,
  folders 34.0–39.6 s, mongo 36.0–37.9 s; folders and mongo tie).
- **No slowdown between iterations:** the C++ harness keeps every iteration (`benchmarks/<service>/cpp-results-pass-*.csv`),
  and mongo's builds with 1 000 books show no trend. The first measured iteration of mongo's batch update with 1 000 books
  was 16–34% slower than the other two in both processes.
- **Not checked:**
  - why the round trip to MongoDB takes 100–230 µs here, against ~50 µs natively on the Mac of the Java run (the virtual
    machine's wake-up latencies are a likely cause);
  - whether the cost per posting of adding to the in-memory sets keeps growing beyond 1 000 books.
- **Estimates:** those for 100 000 and 1 000 000 books extrapolate one or two orders of magnitude. They are useful to compare
  structures and find limits, not as exact predictions.

## 9. Conclusions

Rule: best complexity in the operations that grow with N; on a tie, best constants.

| Component | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Datalake | `batch` | Stores and looks up in O(1), with O(N/1 000) directories. New books are detected with a manifest in O(new) |
| Inverted index | `mongo` | Never worse in complexity than the other two; memory, opening and freshness in O(1). Holds up to ~1.4 million books, but needs bounded documents to keep its updates affordable |
| Metadata | `sqlite` | Same complexity as mongo, 46 times faster and without a server |

Improvements for Stage 2, ordered by impact:

| Improvement | Operation | Now | After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paginate and fetch the page's metadata in one query (`IN`, `$in`), building only the page's results | Metadata of a search | O(R) queries and R result records | 1 query and k records for the k books of the page |
| Store mongo's postings in buckets of bounded size (`{term, bucket, ids}`), adding to the last one | Update a batch | O(T_batch·log V + Σ_touched k·df): 408 ns per touched posting | Independent of N: O(T_batch·log V) and at most B comparisons per new id, with buckets of B ids; no 16 MB limit |
| Intersect starting from the shortest list | Query | O(Σ df·log df) | O(q·df_min) with the lists in memory |
| sqlite: one transaction per batch, and an index on the normalized author or FTS5 | Store and search by author | One synced transaction per book (3.6 ms here); O(N) | One per batch; O(log N + r) |
| Keep each in-memory list as a sorted array instead of a `std::set` (a change for every language, SPEC §8.1 and §14) | Build, update and memory of json | 48 B per posting, one trip to memory per tree level | 4 B per posting, each batch merged in one pass |
| `batch` datalake with a manifest | Detect new books | O(N) | O(new) |
| When several processes or machines index at once, PostgreSQL instead of sqlite for the metadata (section 6.5) | Write metadata concurrently | One writer at a time, on one machine | Many writers on any machine, at one network round trip per query |
| In the longer term, a segment-based index like Lucene's: one immutable segment per batch, background merges and memory-mapped files | Update and query | O(N) per batch | Proportional to the batch's postings, plus O(P·log N) of merges in total, with bounded memory |

## 10. Comparison with the Java analysis

The two analyses reach the same three choices. What changes with C++ and this machine:

- **mongo's updates.** The Java analysis could not measure their cost per posting, which stayed within the noise with
  ≤ 1 000 books, and bounded it with json's. Here it is clear (24.7, 28.5 and 45.2 ms per book with 100, 300 and 1 000
  books, validated within 2.1%), and MongoDB's source explains it: `$addToSet` scans the list for every new id. It is 20
  times json's rewriting, not at most json's: on this machine mongo would need ~31 h to index 100 000 books, the slowest
  of the three.
- **The round trip to MongoDB** costs 100–230 µs in WSL 2, against 47–48 µs on the Mac: mongo's queries and metadata are 3–5
  times slower than in the Java run, and its fixed cost per term dominates every query but the frequent ones with 1 000
  books.
- **folders is much cheaper on ext4 than on APFS** (22–41 µs per term file against ~147 µs; 37 s to build 1 000 books
  against 87 s), but it still does not scale in number of files.
- **The sorted sets of C++ are bound by memory latency.** Adding a posting costs 2–3 µs with 1 000 books and grew 2.7–3.5
  times from 100 books; and in a query, building each result with the constant metadata costs ~30 ns, which makes `mixed`
  cheaper than `frequent`. Neither shows in the Java analysis.
- **json fits more books in memory:** 44 B per posting in the query service, against 63 B in the JVM, and no fixed heap:
  29 GB with 100 000 books against 40 GB.
